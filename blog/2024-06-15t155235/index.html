Learning to program through emulation

Jun 15, 2024
3 min read

So I grew up with the Nintendo Wii and DS. The days of playing Wii Sports, Mario Kart and Super Mario Bros DS will forever hold a special place in my childhood.

The joy of getting a bullet bill during the last lap and the thrill of getting a KO in boxing are some of my most memorable moments in gaming.

Fastforward a couple of years, with the Wii slowing dropping support with the closing of the Wii shop and introduction of the Wii U, there wasn’t really anything to fill my void of Nintendo games (and my parents weren’t willing to buy me another console either).

But I still had a craving for platform-based and retro style games, so I would always surf the internet trying to find games of this style and that’s what led me down the rabbithole of emulation, specifically video game emulation.

The first emulator I tried out was Super Mario World on the zsnes an SNES emulator. Suddenly the joys of my childhood days of gaming came back and it surprised me even more that I didn’t need the actual console to play it.

I was having a lot of fun with this new source of entertainment. I could connect my Redgear gamepad and it could act like an SNES controller. And these programs came with cool features such as screen filters, save states and fastforwarding gameplay.

But one question kept on bugging me. How could a game made for a completely different console be able to run on my laptop? What black magic was happening inside these emulators?

Fastforward to college where I’m completing my Bachelor’s degree in Computer Science. After developing an interest in Computer Architecture I finally decide to write an emulator.

Initially the first emulator I wrote was for the CHIP-8 following this guide in golang. Though the project can be easily done in a week, it was my first time designing the architecture from the ground up. I had always been used to using web frameworks so it was definitely a new experience.

I then had my sights sent on a Gameboy Emulator. However I had grossly underestimated the duration of the project and looking back there were a lot of things I could’ve done differently to speed up the project.

Regardless, after more than a year, I had finally got tetris working. Though some MBC0 games still have graphics issues and I’m yet to add support for memory banking, timing accuracy and sound, it was definitely an incredible learning experience.

If you’re someone who’s curious about how video games work or just programming and computer architecture in general I would definitely recommend trying to write your own emulator as a project.

One of the best things is that it’s language and platform agnostic, so you can write your emulator using in any language as long as it has support for graphics rendering.

Head over to Emudev and r/emudev subreddit, they have a ton of resources to get into emulation developement.

Happy Coding :)
